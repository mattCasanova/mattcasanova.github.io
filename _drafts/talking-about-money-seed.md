## The corrected thesis (Matt, 2026-04-26)

**Top-tier tech comp is NOT a bubble.** Top engineers really do generate millions or billions in value for their companies. Paying them 500K–700K is economically rational — a great bet for the company every single time.

The problem isn't comp inflation. The problem is **expectations.** People started expecting top-tier comp right out of college regardless of whether they were top-tier. When the market got harder, those grads felt the pinch hardest, and the *feeling* of "hiring is broken" spread.

The actual baseline — competent programmer who's willing to do the work can find a job — has always been fine. It just was never easy.

## The V-Shred receipt (anchor anecdote)

- Worked at V-Shred. Built iOS + Android apps with a small team.
- Cost to build, year 1: roughly **$200K** — about 100K each for two devs.
- App revenue, year of launch: roughly **$10 million.**
- $200K in → $10M out. *That's* lopsided.
- That's not "comp inflation." That's a return-on-talent calculation that made paying those engineers a no-brainer.
- **Honesty caveat (Matt's own):** revenue isn't all from the app — backend already existed, marketing was happening, etc. Not claiming the engineers "deserved" all $10M. But the bet of paying $200K to enable $10M revenue is unambiguously good.

V-Shred is already named in [The Guy Whose Name I Don't Remember](/2026/04/14/the-guy-whose-name-i-dont-remember/) and [Bad Advice: Roll Your Own DI](/2026/04/14/bad-advice-roll-your-own-di/) (Kotlin aside). Naming it again is fine.

## Why the "bubble" framing is wrong

Claude (drafting V1) framed top tech comp itself as the bubble. Matt corrected: **it's not.** Top engineers really do produce that much value. The mismatch is in *expectations*, not comp itself.

The corrected story:
- Top engineers' value creation is real and ongoing.
- Comp at the top is rational.
- The mismatch is between **what college grads expect** and **what they can deliver**.
- That mismatch shows up most painfully in down markets, when companies stop hiring marginal candidates and only pay top dollar for top talent.

## Possible angles for the post

1. **Anchor effect on grad expectations.** A small number of FAANG offers reshaped what every CS grad thought they "deserved." Now the market is correcting the *expectations*, not the *comp itself.*
2. **Talent ROI is real.** V-Shred case study + general argument that top engineers really do generate massive multiples of their comp. Companies aren't paying out of the goodness of their hearts; they're making bets that pay back 50x.
3. **The job market has always been hard.** Leetcode is stupid, referrals dominate, networking matters. None of this is new — it just feels new because the easy years are over.
4. **The fear is real, but it's not pay collapsing — it's the gap between expectation and skill being exposed.**

## Original draft text (the cut V1 section — preserved for raw material, framing is wrong)

> Quick aside on why the market feels worse right now even if the underlying baseline isn't broken. This part is going to sound contrarian. Stay with me.
>
> For about 5–10 years, top-tier tech salaries did something weird. Google, Meta, Twitter, Netflix, the rest of the FAANG-adjacent set, started paying *insane* numbers for top engineers. Numbers that didn't make sense if you compared them to almost any other industry. The pay scaled with cost-of-labor at those locations and with how aggressively those companies competed for talent.
>
> The numbers leaked into the culture. They became the *anchor.* Suddenly everybody — including high schoolers picking colleges, including parents giving career advice — was framing "should I learn programming" against the question of whether they'd hit those numbers.
>
> Which meant: people started going to college expecting top-tier comp right out of school. The market filled up with grads anchored on what FAANG paid the top 5%, regardless of whether they were the top 5%.
>
> The current "fear" is partly that bubble popping back to baseline.
>
> The actual baseline — *can a competent programmer who's willing to do the work and adapt find a job in this industry* — is fine. It always has been. It was never easy. Leetcode is dumb. Pipelines have always favored people with referrals. Negotiating is hard. None of that is new. It just feels new because the bubble is over.
