The last post was advice. This one is analysis.

If you're a fresh grad mad about the market, the prescriptive answer is short: *prove them wrong.* Take the bottom rung. Ship receipts. Convert hours into skill, skill into resume, resume into the next offer. Repeat until the market agrees with you. I already wrote that post.

This one is the *why is this happening* version.

The dominant story right now is that tech salaries were a bubble and the bubble is popping. Layoffs, hiring freezes, leetcode getting harder, AI eating junior roles, FAANG bands flattening — every line of that is real, and every line of it is being read as evidence that comp is correcting back to normal.

I want to push back on the framing.

**The bubble was never the pay.**

## Top comp is economically rational

Pretend you're the company for a minute.

You have a product. The product needs engineers. A great engineer ships features that customers actually pay for. A bad engineer ships features that nobody touches and bugs that you eventually have to delete. The difference between those two engineers, on a one-year horizon, can sometimes be the difference between *we built a $10M business* and *we built a tech demo.*

If a great engineer can move the company by a few million dollars a year, what are they worth?

Not *what does it feel polite to pay.* What's the bet?

The bet is straightforward. You pay a lot. You pay $500K. You pay $700K. You pay it every year, gladly, because the alternative is paying $150K for a worse engineer and watching the few million walk out the door.

That's what FAANG comp is. It isn't generosity. It isn't bubble-era exuberance. It's a calculation: *we will pay you a lot of money because we believe — and our data supports — that you will return many multiples of that money.*

[V-Shred receipt goes here — Matt fills in the $200K → $10M math, with the honesty caveat that the backend and the marketing engine were already running. The point isn't that two engineers personally generated $10M of revenue. The point is that the *bet* — $200K of comp to unlock a $10M revenue line — is an unambiguously good bet, and any company that can identify those bets is going to keep making them.]

That's not a bubble. That's an ROI calculation, and ROI calculations are the most stable thing in business. As long as great engineers keep returning multiples of their comp, somebody is going to keep paying them top of market. **The salary number is downstream of the value bet.** The bet is fine. The salary is fine.

## So what was the bubble?

The bubble was in expectations.

For about ten years, those FAANG numbers leaked out of the companies that paid them and into the broader culture. They stopped being *a number paid by a few companies to a few engineers.* They became *the number.* Parents told kids *"go into tech, you'll make $200K."* High schoolers picked colleges around the figure. CS programs filled up with students who had anchored, before they ever wrote a line of code, on the comp at the very top of the ladder.

That's the bubble. Not the pay. **The expectation that the pay was for everybody.**

It wasn't. It never was.

The $200K, $300K, $500K numbers belong to the top few percent of engineers at the top one percent of companies. The number doesn't generalize. It was never going to generalize. But the cultural narrative dropped the asterisk, and an entire generation of CS grads chose the major because they'd been promised a lottery ticket — not because they loved building things.

The market is now pricing them honestly: *you're worth what you can prove.* The gap between what they expected and what they're being offered feels like a collapse. It isn't. It's the cultural anchor finally meeting reality.

## The market was never easy

The other half of the "broken hiring" narrative is that the *process itself* has gotten worse. Leetcode is dumb. Referrals matter more than they should. Negotiating is a skill no one teaches. The whole thing feels gameable in ways that have nothing to do with engineering ability.

All true.

Also: not new.

[Personal beat goes here — Matt grounds this with something from his own path. The pay cut from teaching to programming. The recruiter offers that came in below his teaching salary. Whatever fits. The point is to show that "this was always hard" with a receipt, not just an assertion.]

The job market in 2014 had leetcode. The job market in 2010 had whiteboard algorithms. The job market in 2005 had the same in-group/out-group networking dynamics that fresh grads are complaining about now. None of this is new. It just *feels* new because the window between roughly 2018 and 2022 was an anomaly — the period when bootcamp grads got six-figure offers without grinding leetcode, when junior roles were plentiful, when companies hired aggressively because money was free.

That window was the exception. The grind on either side of it is the rule. We aren't living through a broken hiring process. We're living through the *normal* hiring process, after a brief stretch when normal didn't apply.

## The fear, named

Strip everything else off and the fear underneath the "hiring is broken" narrative is one sentence.

***My skill level isn't what I thought it was.***

That's hard to say. It's a lot easier to say *"the market is broken,"* or *"comp has collapsed,"* or *"AI is taking the junior jobs"* — all of which contain truth, none of which are the load-bearing thing.

The load-bearing thing is the gap. You expected to be worth $200K out of school. The market is offering you $90K, or no offer at all. That gap isn't the market making a mistake. It's the market doing what the market always does — pricing what you can prove.

Which lands us right back at the last post.

## Same conclusion, bigger frame

If the gap is real — and for a lot of people right now, it is — there are still only two ways out of it.

1. Lower your expectations.
2. Close the gap.

I'm not telling you to lower your expectations.

The pay isn't the bubble. The expectation was. The pay is being held up by a real economic engine — top engineers really do generate the value they're paid for. If you want a piece of that engine, you have to climb to where it lives. The path is the same as it's always been. Ship things. Stack receipts. Prove them wrong.

***The bubble was the story, not the salary.***
