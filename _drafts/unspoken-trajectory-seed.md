## The thesis

**The power path in tech runs from engineer to product, and nobody says it out loud.** Every founder-CEO you admire made that walk: Zuckerberg, Musk, Boz, Ben Horowitz (*The Hard Thing About Hard Things* — Matt has reread it many times; Horowitz literally went PM → CEO). Not a coincidence — companies are steered by product decisions, and influence follows the steering wheel. But no manager, no career ladder doc, no mentor ever states it plainly. If your interest runs to architecture, you can go twenty years without anyone telling you which way the ladder actually bends.

---

## The supporting observations

- **Engineering managers don't talk about engineering.** They talk about product. The transition to engineering management is already the transition to a product job — "engineering management is a product job wearing an engineering costume, and nobody tells you until you've already chosen." **[DAY SHIFT — pattern only]**
- **Credit follows visibility.** The IC track (staff/principal) exists as the "credit for invisible work" path, but it only pays for *visibly hard* problems — the novel system, the genius fix. The run-of-the-mill 100-PR slog that made everything actually work earns nothing, because nobody can see what it prevented. (One-line callback to the two-products post here.) → And see the section below: even this track bends toward product above senior.
- **A recent trigger:** a talk by a product manager who used to be an engineer — the pattern keeps surfacing once you look for it. Probably some of the best PMs are ex-engineers.
- **The observation isn't bitter, it's practical:** if the trajectory is real and unspoken, the move is to learn product deliberately instead of discovering the bend at year twenty.

## The IC ladder is also a product ladder (added 2026-08-14 — the counterargument-killer)

The obvious objection to the thesis: "the ladder only bends toward product if you go into management — just take the IC track." This section closes that exit. **[DAY SHIFT — patterns only; the colleague quote stays anonymous]**

- The high-level ICs Matt sees are still product-focused — concerned with features or outcomes, with the assumption the technical part just gets done.
- A staff engineer said it to him directly: the job has been about **influence and direction**, not technical focus. One data point, but it matches everything else he's watched.
- Principal engineers ARE genuinely technical — but as **table stakes, not differentiator**. Nobody gets principal for knowing big-O notation or design patterns better.
- **The structural reason (the analytical core):** genuinely hard engineering problems are few and far between. Most features being rolled out are not massive engineering hurdles. A career ladder can't pay out on rare events — so the everyday currency above senior becomes planning, judgment, influence, direction. Product-shaped skills, on BOTH branches.
- Even the senior promotion isn't "writes better code" or "10x engineer" — it's executing well on features and helping *plan* them. The planning part, not the deep-technical part.
- **Crisp formulation candidates:** "Technical skill is the entry fee, not the differentiator." / "Past senior, the ladder stops measuring how well you write code and starts measuring how well you choose what's worth writing — and how many people you can get to agree."
- **Honesty guardrails (Matt's own, keep them):** not claiming everyone's the same; not claiming principal is unreachable; principals are genuinely technical; junior/senior gaps in raw skill are real. The claim is narrow: what the *differentiator* is above senior.
- **Effect on the thesis:** strengthens it from "one path bends" to "the whole ladder has gravity." The trajectory isn't a fork you can decline by picking the IC branch.

### Why the hard problems are rare (added 2026-08-14 — the mechanism behind the mechanism)

- **They concentrate where most engineers never sit.** Genuinely novel problems live on research teams and truly new surfaces. They exist (they definitely exist at a place like Facebook) — but the average engineer isn't on those teams.
- **Most work is brownfield.** The architecture either already exists and you mirror it, or it doesn't and you do your best to decently architect your one small surface. Re-architecting a large portion is not routine work. Building a feature on top of an existing AI integration, UI/UX screens — there's no hard problem in there to solve.
- **The honest census:** most engineering is building APIs, building UI, or some combination. That's the job.
- **"Hard for us" ≠ "hard" — the V-Shred exhibit (nameable, already named on the blog).** V-Shred felt like wall-to-wall hard problems: APIs, in-app purchases, video streaming, one app doing everything. But nothing was novel to the FIELD — only to the team. Every problem had been solved before; the work was assembling known solutions under pressure. Even work that feels heroic from inside is mostly assembly — which is exactly why it can't be promotion currency.
- **Even novel hardware is mostly resourcefulness, not genius (nameable per carve-out):** on the smart glasses, a hardware limitation shows up and you figure a way around it. Real work, not genius-level problems.
- **Consequence:** if ~90% of the job is mirror-the-architecture assembly, technical depth CAN'T differentiate above senior — ownership and planning are what's left to measure. The rarity isn't a ladder-design flaw; it's the nature of the work, and the ladder just reflects it. (This makes the essay fairer AND darker: nobody rigged it.)

## The personal arc (this post's spine)

- Architecture since ~2006 — game engines onward. Twenty years thinking about it seriously, not just design patterns but whole-app architecture. That was the side that pulled; nobody ever said the ladder bent the other way.
- Now walking the trajectory on purpose: de facto product manager/designer for a real business (the night-shift venture), and teaching himself product from scratch — a deliberate reading curriculum, business books on the treadmill, arguing with *Escaping the Build Trap* mid-run.
- **Mid-learning stance, stated openly:** "I understand I need this. It can't all be engineering. I'm learning it on my own because nobody hands you this." Not a conversion story — he's not renouncing the kitchen. The landing: walk the trajectory *without* agreeing that the second product doesn't matter. Become the person who owns both.
- **The retrospective-obviousness beat (the emotional center — Matt has said "nobody told me" three times now):** looking back it's VERY obvious — managers never once discussed engineering excellence with him as a senior (maybe they do with juniors); everywhere he's worked, big company or small, managers/stakeholders/owners cared about features and product, never the code. Twenty years focused on technical, and the pattern was invisible until it wasn't. Obvious in hindsight, unspoken in real time. Possible refrain for the whole post.
- The AI-era sharpener (optional): with AI handling more of the engineering, the scarce skill shifts toward deciding what to build — the trajectory everyone walked quietly is now being forced on everyone at once.

## Rough outline sketch

1. **Cold open:** rereading *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* for the nth time and noticing, for the first time, the part everyone skips: Horowitz was a product manager. Then the list starts assembling itself — Zuck, Musk, Boz...
2. **The pattern:** the CEO path is engineer → product. Companies steer by product decisions; influence follows the wheel.
3. **Why it's unspoken:** ladders and reviews are written as if engineering depth is the summit; engineering management quietly stops being an engineering job; nobody flags the switch.
   3b. **And the escape hatch is painted on:** the IC track bends the same way above senior — staff runs on influence and direction, principal pays for judgment not deeper CS, because hard technical problems are too rare to run a ladder on. Both branches share one gravity.
4. **The cost of not knowing:** twenty years of architecture love, and the credit system (one-line two-products callback) never paid it the way it pays visible product wins.
5. **Walking it on purpose:** the night-shift business, the reading curriculum, mid-learning honesty.
6. **Landing:** the trajectory is real — walk it with your eyes open, and refuse the false trade: owning product doesn't mean abandoning the kitchen.
