There are two ways to pour a drink.

The jigger: that little steel hourglass bartenders use to measure. One and a half ounces, exactly, every time. It's slower. And it reads wrong from across the bar: the customer watches you measure their drink and sees stingy.

The free pour: bottle straight over the glass, count in your head, done. Faster. Looks generous. And on a Friday night, with the crowd stacked three deep waiting on drinks, no bartender alive reaches for the jigger.

Here's the catch: every free pour runs a little heavy. A quarter ounce here. A splash there. The customer's happy, the bottle looks the same going back on the shelf, and the night's a blur.

The bar finds out what all that generosity cost at inventory, when somebody finally counts what's left in the bottles against what got sold.

If anybody ever counts.

Hold that.

### Cost center

I was talking with somebody about the posts I've been writing here about [the two products](/2026/08/14/tip-the-kitchen/), and they used a term for foundation work. I don't remember the exact phrase. The gist was: that part isn't the money maker. It's a cost center.

I disagreed on the spot. Then I went home and looked it up, because I've learned that when the other side has a word for something and you only have a feeling, [the feeling loses](/2026/06/09/the-code-is-bad-is-not-an-argument/).

Turns out they have a whole dictionary.

**Cost center** is an accounting label. A profit center books revenue; a cost center spends money and books none. Legal, HR, facilities. At a bank, engineering is a cost center. At a software company it's a profit center, and Gergely Orosz [wrote the clean version](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/profit-centers-cost-centers) of that split. But the same knife cuts again inside engineering: features versus foundation. Platform, infra, tests. The stuff that doesn't ship a screen.

Geoffrey Moore calls it [core versus context](https://blog.roadmap.one/posts/blog6-8-core-vs-context/): core is what customers pick you for, context is everything else you're forced to do. Ops calls it [KTLO](https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/keeping-the-lights-on-ktlo-meaning/), keep the lights on. SAFe calls it an enabler. Werner Vogels called it [undifferentiated heavy lifting](https://www.teleportsignal.com/articles/undifferentiated-heavy-lifting/) and built AWS on the phrase.

Five names for the same idea: necessary, unloved, not on the menu. ([What that filing does to the people doing the work](/2026/09/03/the-unposted-rule/) is its own post.)

And underneath the names, a rule. Under US GAAP ([ASC 350-40](https://www.neo.tax/blog/how-to-capitalize-software-costs-asc-350-40-explained)), building new functionality gets *capitalized*. It's an asset. It sits on the balance sheet and depreciates over years. Maintenance, bug fixes, refactoring get *expensed*. They hit this quarter's books in full.[^asu]

Read that again. The feature is an investment. The foundation under it is a cost. Not because anybody at your company decided that. An accounting rule decided it, years ago, before anyone walked into your planning meeting.

So when somebody calls foundation work a cost center, they aren't being dumb. They're reading the ledger.

It's just that the ledger is fucking wrong.

### The free variable

Here's why, and it isn't villains.

Kent Beck laid it out in *Extreme Programming Explained*, back in 1999. Four variables in any project: scope, time, cost, quality. Fix any three and the fourth moves. The business fixes time (the deadline). The roadmap fixes scope. Headcount fixes cost.

Quality is what's left.

And it's the only one of the four whose damage is invisible this quarter.

Blow the deadline and everyone knows Friday.

Cut scope and the customer notices.

Let quality float and the release looks fine, the demo looks fine, the dashboard looks fine.

Beck's own warning was that quality is a terrible variable to let float, precisely because it looks free right up until the shit hits the fan.

That's the free pour. The deadline is the Friday-night crowd, stacked three deep. You don't reach for the jigger. Every skipped test is a quarter ounce over. The bottle looks the same.

And the cost doesn't vanish when quality floats. It moves off the books.

Onto somebody devs evenings or weekend.

Onto next quarter, as rot.

Onto the customer, as bugs.

The bar is still paying for the heavy pour. It's paying in liquor it never charged for.

### The other side, honestly

I went looking for the argument against me, expecting "code quality doesn't matter." Nobody serious says that. What they say is sharper, and I owe it a fair pour.

**The future has to exist for future velocity to pay.** Foundation is a claim on next year's speed, discounted by the odds you're still around next year. At ten percent survival odds, "we'll ship faster later" is worth a dime on the dollar. I've made that trade myself and [called it good debt](/2026/06/09/the-code-is-bad-is-not-an-argument/). The startup canon is right for its stage.

**Foundation built ahead of need is a bet on a future you predicted.** Sandi Metz: ["duplication is far cheaper than the wrong abstraction."](https://sandimetz.com/blog/2016/1/20/the-wrong-abstraction) Netscape threw away a working browser to rebuild it clean and never recovered; Spolsky wrote [the obituary](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/04/06/things-you-should-never-do-part-i/). The most expensive failures in this industry were quality-motivated. A manager who discounts the engineer's rewrite pitch by default is applying a rational prior.

**Quality can't be priced, so it can't be a product.** No customer can see it. Engineers can't even agree what it is: Casey Muratori's [Clean Code, Horrible Performance](https://www.computerenhance.com/p/clean-code-horrible-performance) is a senior engineer calling another senior engineer's foundation gold-plating. You can't fund a contested, unmeasurable category. So fund outcomes and make quality the engineer's job inside them. That's Cagan's position, and it's Perri's, and I agreed with both of them [when I told you to tip the kitchen](/2026/08/14/tip-the-kitchen/).

The other side doesn't say *never*. It says: not yet, not that much, and not on your say-so.

That's a better argument than "cost center." It's also the one I was actually losing to, every time, at every place I've worked. Because "make it the engineer's job inside the feature" only works if the feature's budget has room for it, and the deadline never does.

### Where I'm pouring from

I should say where I'm pouring from, because it's a soft spot.

I have a day job. The night shift is [building something for later](/2026/07/22/the-rate-went-up/), and it has no customers yet. People are starting to play with the apps, but nobody's stacked three deep at my bar. So the horizon argument doesn't bite me the way it bites a startup on six months of runway, and I won't pretend it does. That's a luxury. I'm naming it.

A few days that turned into a few weeks, on and off, tweaking my AWS instance templates until a fresh box pulls the provisioning scripts out of git on its own and builds itself. Every one-off fix goes back into the scripts, because a one-off I didn't write down is a bug I scheduled for later. It's the work you'd pay a DevOps person for. Build versus buy, and the build is me, Claude, and a folder of shell scripts.

The easy version was one instance and a shell history. Ad hoc. Every startup does it, and every startup is right to.

Then I had to rebuild the instances. A few times: free tier to paid, plus one thing I'd call a disaster, depending on how you count.[^disaster] Reprovisioning took about fifteen minutes. Not zero. Fifteen.

You can call that a waste until there's a customer. Maybe it is. But the bottle's been on the scale once already, and I know what the pour cost.

### Weigh the bottles

Here's what the bar industry figured out that mine still argues about.

The loss from a heavy pour is invisible per drink and fatal per year. So bars don't guess. They weigh the bottles. Every night, or every week, somebody puts each bottle on a scale, compares what's left to what was sold, and gets a number called pour cost. Not because the bartender is a thief. Because the loss is slow, small, and nobody sees it happen.

Software got told it can't be measured. Some of it has been.

Martin Fowler drew [the graph](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/DesignStaminaHypothesis.html) in 2007 and [wrote the essay](https://martinfowler.com/articles/is-quality-worth-cost.html) in 2019: internal quality lowers the cost of features, and the payback is weeks, not years. Read it. Then notice he admits it's a hypothesis. He had no data.

Other people went and got some.

Adam Tornhill and Markus Borg put 39 production codebases on the scale in [*Code Red*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.04374) (2022): thirty thousand files, defects and cycle times pulled from real issue trackers. Low-quality code carried **fifteen times** the defects of high-quality code. Fixing an issue in it took **124 percent longer** on average. And the worst-case fix times ran **nine times** longer, which is the one that should scare anyone who plans a roadmap. It isn't just slower. It's unpredictable.

Then AI showed up and made the test cheap, and this is where the bottle really gets weighed.

DORA's [2024 report](https://dora.dev/insights/balancing-ai-tensions/) found that every 25 percent increase in AI adoption came with a 1.5 percent *drop* in delivery throughput and a 7.2 percent drop in stability. The [2025 report](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report) saw throughput turn positive, instability persist, and landed on a phrase: AI is an amplifier. It magnifies whatever discipline the org already had.

GitClear read [211 million lines](https://www.gitclear.com/ai_assistant_code_quality_2025_research) of commits. Refactoring (code that got *moved*) fell from 24 percent of changes in 2020 to under 10 percent in 2024. Copy-pasted lines rose by half. Duplicated blocks rose eightfold. 2024 was the first year on record that copy-paste beat refactoring.

And METR ran [a randomized trial](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) on experienced open-source maintainers with early-2025 tools: they were 19 percent slower with AI, and afterward believed they'd been 20 percent faster.[^metr]

More code. Less refactoring. More duplication. Same or worse stability. And the people pouring think they're doing great.

The price of a test fell to almost nothing. The budget for it still didn't appear. That's the cleanest evidence you'll get that "we can't afford quality" was never about cost. Quality was the free variable before the machine showed up. The machine just turned the crank.

### What still can't be weighed

I'll give the other side its last point, because it's the one that keeps winning.

The number I actually want, the one that would end the meeting, is *how much faster would this feature have shipped on a clean base.* That number has no instrument. It's a counterfactual. You can measure complexity. You can measure dead code. You can measure cycle time trending up, rework rate, change failure rate, hotspots where complexity and churn overlap (which is what Tornhill's tooling does). Those are proxies. They tell you the pour is heavy. They don't tell you which drink.

I said it can't be measured. Half wrong. It can, partly, and the field did the weighing. I just never had the numbers in the room. What I was right about is that the number I had is never the number the deadline is measured in. The dashboard the date lives on has no row for it. Everyone says quality matters. Judge them by the budget, because that's the only number in the building that isn't a stated preference.

None of this is mine. Beck named the variable in 1999. Fowler drew the graph in 2007. Tornhill counted in 2022. I'm the guy at the rail reading it back in my own words. That's what a bar is for.

Jigger or free pour, the drink gets poured either way. Nobody's arguing for empty glasses. The only question is whether anyone puts the bottle on the scale before the year's over.

Weigh the bottles.

See you, space cowboy.

[^disaster]: One. Maybe. It depends how generous you are with the word. No customers were affected, on account of there not being any yet.
