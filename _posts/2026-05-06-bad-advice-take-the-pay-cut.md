I work at Meta. Before that I taught video game programming at the university level for seven years. I have former students who worked at Meta, TikTok, Bungie, Microsoft, half a dozen game studios I'd have to look up to remember them all.

Before any of that, I installed mirrors and shower doors on construction sites. I had a two-year degree, because I didn't feel like spending two more years in school. I just wanted to earn money. So I quit and took the construction job. A year in I realized I wasn't going to make what I wanted there.

Everybody thinks they're smarter than their boss. I was in that bucket.

I wanted a boss I could actually learn from. So I went back to school, finished a new four-year degree, and became a programmer.

That was 2005. **Same bet on myself every job since. Prove them wrong.**

Knowing where I started and where I am now, I get shocked at students who did mediocre in college, graduate, can't get the offer they want, and then go online to complain that the market won't pay them $200K out of school.

The cultural assumption: *programmers make a lot of money. I learned to code, I should get paid like a programmer. Right?*

I have an opinion about that.

I took a pay cut going from teaching to programming.

I'd [co-authored a book on game programming patterns](https://www.amazon.com/Game-Development-Patterns-Best-Practices-ebook/dp/B01MRP7SPA/). I came back to America thinking the resume read like *seven years of senior-ish work with a teaching layer on top.*

It didn't.

*"Lecturer, DigiPen, 7 years"* read like *"has never had a real job."* My recruiter brought me job offers that were *lower than I was making as a teacher.*

I took an entry-level junior offer.

It didn't matter what I thought I was worth.

**You don't get paid for what you think you're worth. You get paid for what you can prove.**

## My students earned more than me - Good for them

While I was teaching in Korea I taught freshmen and sophomores — the first two years of a four-year game development degree. My students would graduate, fly to America to finish, and within a couple of years be making more money than I was.

That's fine. They were smart. They earned it. I genuinely don't have a take on that one. Good for them.

The part I'm talking about is when the *fresh* graduate goes online and is mad they aren't making $200K out of school.

The kid is competing with senior developers. Same job postings, same recruiters, same hiring managers picking between résumés. *That's* the problem. They're in a fight they can't win — not yet. Five years from now, maybe. Twelve years from now, sure. Today they have a degree and zero shipped receipts. The $200K number they saw online belongs to someone who's been proving it for a decade.

That kid's argument is: *"I know I'm worth that."*

OK. Find the company that agrees and take the offer.

*If you can.*

If you can't — if no company in your city, in your time zone, in your industry, in *the entire labor market* will pay you the number you've decided you're worth — then you have a **market problem.** The market is, *right now,* setting your price. And the price isn't what you think it should be.

Two ways to handle that:

1. Lower your expectations.
2. **Prove the market wrong.**

I'm not telling you to lower your expectations.

## Five Apps, Three Percent

I took the junior offer in Vegas. A registered-agent services company that wanted mobile apps. Hired me as the Android developer.

A month in, the iOS developer quit on a Friday.

I now owned both apps. Different apps, by the way — not one cross-platform codebase. Two completely separate native apps, two languages, two release pipelines. I was hired to be the Android dev about a month earlier. I was now also an iOS dev.

Then we moved everything to **Ionic** so we could share code with the web team in Angular. I built the whole rebrand pipeline — one Ionic codebase, but the company brand-labeled apps for different sub-companies, so each build target had to swap logos, URLs, theme colors, the whole package. By the end of about eighteen months I'd shipped **five apps and an Angular web tool** out of that one codebase. The business loved it. The work was visible. My name was on it.

I asked for a raise.

I got **3%.**

## Prove them wrong

That's the line. Tattoo it.

You ship five apps in eighteen months, you know what you brought, you ask for a raise that reflects it, and you get three percent. There are exactly two ways to read that:

1. *I'm being underpaid for my skills.* (maybe true)
2. *Therefore my employer is obligated to pay me more.* (Not how the world works)

Reality check. The market does not owe you anything. Your employer is just signaling what *their* bid is — what they're willing to pay you to stay. If your skills are actually worth more *somewhere else,* the only way to find out is to go check.

Was 3% what I was worth? I didn't think so. Maybe I hadn't earned more yet. Either way, only one way to find out.

I went and checked.

Got an offer. **+$15K.** Took it. Hired as the Android developer. Started on a Monday. The iOS developer's two weeks had ended the Friday before — nobody told me. Day one I was the iOS *and* Android developer at this company too. Nobody at the company knew the projects. I still crushed it, and started learning react and laravel on the side to help out the web team. I shipped a lot of stuff. My name was on it. The business loved it.

And I'd grown — I could manage two apps and lead the iOS dev we eventually hired. New skills.

Time to check the market again.

Got another offer. **+$10K.** Took it. Crushed it again. Eventually got the job at Meta, which is the one I'm in now.

From the pay cut to Meta: about four years. From the pay cut to *quadruple* what I was making that first day in America: also about four years.

Nobody owed me a six-figure salary. I had to prove it.

**I still do, every day.**

That's the rule:

> ***Prove them wrong.***

## You paid $100K for school - Now they're paying *you*

Here's the part that's going to make me sound like the guy three whiskies in, yelling at the bar.

You took out loans. You paid forty grand a year. You sat in classes. You did projects. **Nobody saw those projects.** Your teacher saw them. Your TA saw them. The kid at the next desk saw them. Maybe you put them on a portfolio site that had three visitors and one of them was your mom.

You did this — willingly, eagerly, on credit — for **four years.**

Then a company offers you a paying job at the bottom of the ladder. They will *pay you* to work on real products that real customers use, and the work you do **goes on your resume.** You won't have to make up screenshots for a portfolio. You'll have shipped apps.

And somewhere on the internet, somebody is *complaining about staying an extra hour.*

Are you fucking kidding me.

You paid six figures to do projects nobody cared about. They are paying *you* to do projects that **literally get you the next job.**

You are still in school.

**But now you get a paycheck.**

It is the cheapest, fastest tuition you will ever get, and the cash flows the other direction. You are being paid for it.

Show up early.

I went in on Saturdays at that first job. [Literal YouTube proof](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5V5B-CBVwd0) — a video I called *Work to Learn,* filmed eight years ago, walking into the office at 4am on a Saturday morning after the gym. I was first in, every day. By the time I got to V-Shred a couple jobs later, the owner gave me a key because I kept beating him to the office and he was tired of me waiting around outside.

Eight years on, I'm still doing it.

**But now I just get paid more.**

That's not heroism. That's *math.* Every extra hour I put in, I was trading for skill, which I was going to convert to higher pay at this job or the next. Hour for skill, skill for resume, resume for offer, offer for raise.

## On unpaid internships, while we're here

Same argument, different scale.

I never did one. Lucky me. But I see people online absolutely *furious* about the existence of unpaid internships. *"Don't work for free." "Get paid what you're worth."*

Sure. *If* you can find a company that agrees with your number, take that. Take the highest offer you have. Always.

If you *can't,* and a company is offering to let you ship real work in exchange for real-world experience that goes on your resume — they are paying you in the only currency you don't have yet. The currency you can't buy and can't fake. The one only experience produces.

If you're already pulling $200K a year, fine, don't do free internships. You can't afford to. You're past the point. Charge for it.

If you can't get the first job, and somebody is offering to teach you using their codebase as the textbook? **Take it.** Build the thing. Make the resume. Get the next offer. The internship *is* paying you — just in something other than dollars. Eight years from now that experience will compound back into your salary with interest.

Seth Godin keeps saying the same thing in *Linchpin* — make art, give gifts.

The art isn't paint on canvas. The gift isn't wrapped under a tree.

***The art is proving your worth. The gift is showing up when you don't have to.***

Intern, junior, senior, staff — the pay grade changes. The posture doesn't.

## Higher pay means higher stakes

I want to be careful about this part, because the easy read is *"oh great, this guy made it to a big company, now he gets to lecture the rest of us about hustle."*

The reality is, **The stakes are higher, not lower.**

There are a thousand versions of this idea written by a thousand different people:

- *The only easy day was yesterday.*
- *Who's gonna carry the boats?*
- *Discipline equals freedom.*
- *Every day is day one.*
- *Stay hungry. Stay foolish.*
- *Ego is the enemy.*
- *The obstacle is the way.*

They all point at the same posture: the day you decide you've arrived is the day someone else is still showing up. Big tech does not pay a lot of money to people who've taken their foot off the gas. They pay it because every day they expect you to bring back more than you cost. The day you stop doing that is the day you become a line item on somebody's spreadsheet.

Here's another example: Meta has done layoffs every year since the end of 2022.

Early 2025, we knew one was coming. I was sitting in the office on a Friday next to a teammate. I asked if he was coming in over the weekend — because that's typically what we both did, because [we noticed and we cared](/2026/05/01/think-mcfly-think/).

He was unsure. *Going in the weekend right before a layoff is the kind of thing that feels stupid if you get laid off anyway.* Fair point. I agreed.

Then I said: *"yeah, but if we don't get laid off, we just wasted two days before launch."*

He came in. We both came in. We worked the weekend.

Neither of us got laid off.

Was the weekend the reason I didn't get laid off? Probably not on its own. But the math wasn't hard. The cost of showing up if it didn't matter: a Saturday. The cost of not showing up if it did: my job. Easy call.

According to the news, another big layoff is coming in a few weeks. Will I work the weekend before? **Yes, if that's what shipping needs.** I am not going to waste days before a deadline because somebody upstairs may or may not have already decided I'm cut.

If I get laid off, I can rest then.

Actually — scratch that.

**If I get laid off, my job becomes finding a new one.**

## Last call

You don't get to decide your pay. The market does.

If the market won't pay you what you think you're worth, that's not a market problem. It's a *you* problem. Your resume hasn't proven it yet.

Take the job anyway. There's a line in *Rich Dad Poor Dad* — whatever you think about that book — that's worth keeping: **the rich don't work for money. They work to learn.**

I called that YouTube video *Work to Learn* eight years ago for a reason. The pay comes after.

Show up Saturday.

Crush it harder than the person online complaining the pay is too low.

Ship things with your name on them. Stack the receipts. Walk into the next interview with a folder full of *I built that* and walk out with a raise.

If your current employer won't pay you what you're worth, **the next one will.** That's the whole game. The market has a price for your skills. You raise that price by *proving them wrong.* You don't raise it by tweeting that the pay is too low.

***Prove them wrong.***
